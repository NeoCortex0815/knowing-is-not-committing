Abstract

MIT's weight-space edit reduces reasoning loops in Qwen3.5-0.8B. We rebuilt that edit independently, wrote a truth-gated reflex into 16 MLP neurons, and ran a series of pre-registered tests on fresh MATH problems. The model often knows a correct answer long before it commits to it: a linear probe reads the truth inside the reasoning, yet the post-trained model delivers only a small part of the correct values that appear in its thinking. Without its thinking block (answer mode), the same model is strong, and when two independent answer-mode runs agree, most of their answers are right. Sampling settings alone move MIT's own edit from 25 % to 55 %. Every reading was fixed in a dated log before the run, and refuted readings are reported alongside the confirmed ones.

Findings

  • Overthinking. With thinking on, 90 % of the post-trained model's runs on 50 fresh MATH problems end in a loop stop or at the 32K-token cap, and it solves 5 of 50. The pre-trained base loops in 20 % and solves 27.
  • Knowing without committing. In 24 of those 50 runs the correct value appears in the thinking, but the model delivers it only 5 times. An independent count on all 500 MATH-500 problems found the same gap.
  • A council of answer-mode runs. Our rule B+H lets several runs without thinking answer first and calls a thinking run only when they disagree. In a test fixed publicly in advance it solved 77 of 150 fresh problems, against 67 for MIT's edit with thinking.
  • New in version 0.5. An independent check of SGNE-CC, a weight edit by Igor M. Zhuk, with ablations built from its released tensors: its GatedDeltaNet part carries most of the effect. With SGNE-CC as the thinking back room, the council in its lean form beats MIT's edit on 100 fresh problems (59 vs 49) at about 70 % of its tokens per correct answer. At this size the better intervention is his, not ours.
